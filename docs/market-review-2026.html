Our Planner Against the Market

What our travel planner does better than the apps people can download today, where it plainly loses, and the twelve things worth doing next — written for someone who has never read a line of its code.

Reviewed against the live app at travel-planner-3e0d3.web.app and against eleven competing products · no figures in this report are estimates of our own usage, because the app has none yet

If you read nothing else

  • We have built a very good travel companion and almost no travel salesman. Once a trip is in the app, nothing on the market handles the actual days better — two clock times per stop, free-time lanes, sub-routes that recompute when you drag them, spending, packing and a diary that all work with the phone in aeroplane mode. Every rival here needs a signal for something.
  • Our weakest moment is the first five minutes. Competitors fill a trip for you — from your inbox, from a chat prompt, from a database of places with photos and ratings. We hand a new person a fictional demo city and a paste box. That is the single biggest reason someone would try us and leave.
  • Nobody can copy our promise, and we barely make it. Free forever, no subscription, no account required, works with no signal, and it never changes your plan behind your back. Wanderlog charges £/$40 a year for offline access and dark mode; the AI planners quietly invent opening hours. We should be saying this out loud on a first screen, and we say it nowhere.

01What we actually built

In plain words, for anyone joining the conversation now.

It is a trip app you add to your phone's home screen straight from the browser — there is no App Store, no download, no sign-up wall. Open it and you get five tabs for one trip: a Map of the day, a Plan that lists the day hour by hour, a Shop list that turns into a spending report, a Prep list for packing, and a Log for notes and photos as the trip happens.

The part that makes it different from a notes app is the middle of the day. A stop has a start time and an end time, so the gaps between the tour's stops are real, visible lanes of free time. Inside a gap you can build your own little side trip — three shops and back to the coach by 15:45 — and the app does the arithmetic: leave at this time, you have this much slack, here is the walk. It also nags you once, quietly, when it is time to leave.

Everything is stored on the phone first, so every screen works underground, on a plane or in a mountain village. Before you go you can draw a box on the map and keep that patch of map on the phone too. Sharing a trip hands somebody a copy, not a live document: what they change stays theirs until one of you presses Send an update, and then the other side approves the changes one at a time. Nothing rewrites your day while you are asleep.

02Our app, honestly

Green is what genuinely works. Amber is what a new user would hit.

What we do better than anyone

  • It works with no signal — all of itNot "cached recently". The whole app reads from the phone, and map tiles are the one thing you fetch in advance, on purpose, over an area you draw.
  • The day is modelled properlyTwo clock times per stop, free time as its own lane, side trips with a real deadline and live arithmetic. No competitor treats the gaps in a guided tour as first-class.
  • Money that matches realityYou list what you want to buy, tick it when you do, type what you really paid, and get spend by day, by payment method, and how wrong your estimates were.
  • Sharing that cannot surprise youA shared trip is a copy. Updates arrive as a list of proposals you accept one by one, and your own edits are never offered back to you as someone else's deletions.
  • It tells the truth when it failsUnsent changes get a plain screen naming what is stuck, why, and what would be lost — instead of a spinner and a lie about being safe.
  • Nothing is monetising youNo adverts, no booking commissions steering results, no subscription, no data sold. What is on the screen is what you put there.

Where it falls down

  • Getting your trip in is workYou paste text and correct rows. Rivals read your email inbox, or build a week from one sentence. Our first screen is a fictional demo city, which reads as "not for my trip".
  • It cannot suggest anywhereThere is no database of places with photos, ratings and opening hours. If you do not already know where you are going, the app cannot help you decide.
  • Reminders only work while it is openThe "leave now" nudge lives inside the app. Close it and the phone stays silent — which is exactly when you needed telling.
  • No live planning with other peopleThe snapshot model is safer, but two friends editing a trip on the sofa together is a normal thing to want, and we make them take turns.
  • English, light mode, one screen sizeAnything typed in Chinese, Japanese or Korean is untested against the layout. There is no dark mode at all — a nightly annoyance and a feature rivals charge for.
  • Nobody can find itNo App Store listing, no website, no reviews to read, no support address. For most travellers, an app that is not in a store does not exist.
  • One person's projectNo privacy policy or terms on the site, no support channel, and if the one Firebase account lapses the links stop working. A group tour will not trust that with eight days of plans.

03What people actually use today

The market splits into five families. Almost nobody uses one app.

Travellers take about 71 days from first idea to booking — roughly a month being inspired and five weeks researching — and most of them end up juggling two or three apps rather than settling on one. Google Maps, with over two billion monthly users, is the real incumbent: its saved Lists are how an enormous number of people "plan" a trip, and its multi-stop planner handles a large share of long-distance route queries. Any new planner is competing with a folder of Google Maps pins and a spreadsheet, not only with other planners.

The five families are: organisers that tidy bookings you already made (TripIt); planners that combine a map, an itinerary and collaboration (Wanderlog, Lambus, Tripsy); AI chat planners that generate a trip from a prompt and want to sell you the booking (Mindtrip, Layla); journals that record where you went (Polarsteps); and specialists that do one job well (PackPoint for packing, TravelSpend for money, Sygic for offline maps). We sit between the planners and the journals, and we are the only one that treats the middle of a guided day as the problem.

04The competition, app by app

Prices and behaviour as reported in 2026 reviews; each entry ends with the one thing worth taking from it.

Wanderlog

Free · Pro $39.99/yr

The closest thing to a direct rival, and the app most people mean by "trip planner".

Strong
  • Clean, well-liked interface; itinerary, map, budget and reservations in one place
  • Real-time collaboration — several people editing one trip, which is why groups pick it
  • A big places database with photos and recommendations, so a blank trip fills fast
  • Generous free tier; the paid tier adds route optimisation and Google Maps export
Weak
  • Offline access is behind the $39.99 subscription — and so is dark mode
  • Still a lot of manual typing; the free AI help is capped at a handful of messages per trip
  • No real-time flight alerts for frequent flyers
  • Reviewers call the full feature set overwhelming for a simple trip

Take from itOffline is our free, permanent feature and their paid one. That is a headline, and we have never printed it.

TripIt

Free · Pro $49/yr

The veteran organiser: forward a confirmation email, get an itinerary.

Strong
  • Unbeatable at the one job — reads booking emails and builds the schedule with no typing
  • Pro adds live flight delay, gate and cancellation alerts and fare tracking
  • Works on phone, tablet and desktop, and syncs into your calendar
Weak
  • It only starts after you have booked — useless for deciding what to do on Tuesday
  • Interface widely described as dated; one review calls it "enterprise software trapped in 2012"
  • No real collaboration, no in-app booking, and document uploads are capped (3 free, 25 on Pro)

Take from itZero-typing import is the feature people pay for. We cannot read email, but a calendar-invite (.ics) import would cover flights and hotels for free.

Mindtrip & Layla — the AI planners

Free, paid by bookings

Describe your trip in a sentence, get a full week back in seconds.

Strong
  • The blank page problem simply disappears — a draft week in under a minute
  • Mindtrip can complete a real flight booking inside the chat, and is good at group and family context
  • Layla adds proactive warnings for family travel; both feel effortless to start
Weak
  • They invent things. Reviewers report attractions that no longer exist and time arithmetic that does not add up — one tested planner silently assumed the wrong travel year for pricing
  • Free to you because a booking commission is the business model, so suggestions are not neutral
  • Everything runs in the cloud: no signal, no plan

Take from itTheir speed is real and their honesty is not. Our paste importer already grades every row and lands nothing until you confirm — that is the right shape for any AI we ever add.

Polarsteps

Free · paid photo books

A travel diary that draws itself from your phone's location.

Strong
  • Records the journey automatically and beautifully; family follow along on a live map
  • Tracks offline and syncs later — the one rival that shares our offline instinct
  • Added a free AI trip planner in 2025 that learns from your own past trips
Weak
  • Barely plans: no prices, no route optimisation, little day-by-day structure
  • Users report persistent bugs and missing basics such as merging trips
  • Background GPS tracking is a real privacy and battery cost

Take from itOur Log is a better diary than people realise, and we never show it off. A shareable trip recap is the cheapest delight in this report.

Google Maps Lists

Free

Not a planner, and the thing most people use instead of one.

Strong
  • Already installed, already trusted, two billion users a month
  • Every place on earth with hours, photos, reviews and live directions
  • Offline map areas are built in and free
Weak
  • A list of pins is not a day: no times, no gaps, no packing, no spending, no notes
  • Sharing a list gives no structure and no way to agree on changes
  • Falls apart the moment a trip has eight days and three people

Take from itWe should import and export to it rather than fight it. "Open in Google Maps" already exists on our stops; a list import would remove an hour of typing.

Tripsy, Lambus, Sygic Travel & the specialists

$3–$5 one-off, or free

Smaller, sharper tools that each own one corner.

Strong
  • Tripsy: a clean one-off purchase, no subscription — proof people will pay once for a calm app
  • Lambus: collaborative planning as the whole point, popular with groups
  • Sygic Travel: offline city maps and walking tours, the most travel-proof when data dies
  • PackPoint (packing, from your weather and activities) and TravelSpend (currencies, budgets, exports) each do one of our tabs better than our tab does
Weak
  • Tripsy is Apple-only, so half the world cannot use it
  • Sygic's planning is described as clunky next to the polished apps
  • The specialists do not talk to each other: your packing list lives in one app and your trip in another

Take from itOur advantage over the specialists is that packing, spending and the plan are the same trip. That is worth saying plainly, because it is invisible from a screenshot.

06So where do we stand

We win outright on three things. Nothing else runs a whole trip with no signal for free. Nothing else models a guided day — fixed stops with your own free time between them — at all, and that is the exact shape of a package tour, which is a very large slice of travel in Asia. And nothing else refuses to change your plan without asking; every collaborative rival will happily let someone else's edit land on your Tuesday.

We lose outright on two. The first five minutes, where rivals fill a trip from an inbox or a sentence and we show a demo of a city that does not exist. And discovery — both senses: the app cannot suggest a restaurant, and nobody can find the app. Those two are what would keep our user count at one.

The good news is that neither of the losses requires abandoning anything we decided. Everything below is additive, and none of it costs a subscription to run.

07Twelve things to do next

Ordered as a route, not a wish list. The first four are the ones that change whether a stranger keeps the app.

Now — the first five minutes

  1. Replace the demo city with "what is your trip?"

    A new person currently lands in a fictional place called Meridian City. Make the first screen ask for a name, a date and where you are going — then offer the demo as a clearly-labelled "have a look around first". The trip that belongs to them should exist within thirty seconds.

    SmallFixes the biggest drop-off

  2. Make importing a real itinerary feel like magic, not homework

    The paste box works and grades every line honestly — keep that. What is missing is the way in: accept a shared file or a screenshot's text from the phone's share sheet, read calendar invites (.ics) from airlines and hotels so flights need no typing at all, and import a Google Maps saved list of pins. Three free inputs that between them cover how people already hold a trip.

    MediumCloses the gap to TripIt

  3. Say the promise on the way in

    One honest line before the first screen: works with no signal, no account, no subscription, never changes your plan behind your back. Wanderlog charges forty dollars a year for the first of those. Right now a visitor has no way to know we give it away.

    TinyPositioning

  4. Let the "leave now" reminder reach a locked phone

    The nudge is the most useful thing the app knows and it only fires while the app is open. Web push notifications now work on an installed iPhone home-screen app, so this is reachable; if it proves unreliable, exporting the day to the phone's own calendar gets most of the value for almost no work.

    MediumThe feature people would tell friends about

Next — the trip itself

  1. Dark mode

    Closed once as "light only, for now". Worth reopening: half of trip planning happens in bed and half of trip using happens on a night coach. Wanderlog sells it. The palette is already defined in one place, so this is a contained job rather than a redesign.

    MediumDaily comfort

  2. Survive Chinese, Japanese and Korean text

    Not a translation project — a robustness pass. People will paste Japanese itineraries into an app whose warnings are built English sentences and whose rows assume Latin widths. For a Malaysian traveller in Japan this is not a nicety.

    MediumWhole markets

  3. Real travel times instead of stored guesses

    Nearby places show fixed figures written when the data was made. Free, keyless routing services can return an honest walking or transit time per leg, with the stored figure kept as the offline fallback. The sub-route arithmetic is only as trustworthy as its inputs.

    MediumTrust in the maths

  4. Money for groups: who paid, and who owes whom

    The spending report is already better than most rivals', but a package tour splits taxis and dinners. Recording who paid and settling up at the end would remove the second app people run alongside a planner, and it fits the data we already keep.

    MediumRemoves a rival app

  5. Make the shared trip check for updates by itself

    Keep the model — a copy, approved one change at a time, is our best idea. Just stop making people remember: check quietly, and show a badge saying "three changes waiting". That is most of what live collaboration feels like, with none of the risk.

    SmallCloses the group gap

  6. Offline maps that cover a whole city

    Today an area is capped at fifteen hundred map tiles, because it would be wrong to bulk-download a charity's servers. A modern single-file offline map format would let one download cover a region, legitimately and still for free — turning our best feature from "a patch around your hotel" into "Japan is on your phone".

    LargeWidens our strongest lead

Later — bets worth considering, not decided

  1. An assistant that proposes and never writes

    If AI ever enters this app, it should arrive through the paste screen's door: every suggestion graded, nothing landing until a human ticks it, and a visible mark on anything a machine wrote. That is the opposite of the tools that quietly send people to closed restaurants, and it would be a genuine market position rather than a catch-up feature.

    LargeDifferentiation, if done honestly

  2. Be findable, and be trustworthy about it

    A one-page site that explains the app, a support address, a plain-language privacy note, and a finished screen-reader pass. None of it is glamorous; all of it is what a stranger checks before putting eight days of plans into an app made by someone they have never met.

    SmallMakes everything else reachable

08Three things not to do

Every one of these would trade our advantage for parity

  • Do not switch to live shared editing. It is the one thing everyone else has and the one thing our users are protected from. Make the copy-and-approve model faster instead — item 9 above.
  • Do not take booking commissions. The moment a suggestion earns money, every suggestion is suspect, which is precisely the charge reviewers level at the AI planners.
  • Do not add a paid API to solve a hard problem. The no-paid-services rule is why the app can be free forever and why it has no server to fail. Every improvement above was chosen because a free route exists.
