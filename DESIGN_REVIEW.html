  • 31 Firebase is stood up properly, with a real sign-in. The model, as built Items 25, 04, 05 and 06, all settled — draw against this rather than around it.

There is one kind of record. A place is a name, a position, a category and whatever OpenStreetMap knows about it. A visit is that place on a day, at a time, on a route — which is what the itinerary shows and what everyone calls a stop. A place that hangs off another place is what the Nearby list shows. Same record, same screen, same creation form.

place name · position · category · hours, phone, website · source link │ ├── anchored to another place, or nothing if it stands alone ├── nearby every place anchored to this one ├── must-see shots to get here ├── shopping what to buy here └── notes one per place per day — item 04, now built day ├── visits ordered: place + start + length → end is derived └── loops as many as the day has gaps — item 05, now built start place · end place · places in between depart · return by → time to spend

Two consequences worth designing to. A place saved from a Nearby list is already a candidate stop — it needs no retyping, only a day and a time. And shopping, shots, notes and the loops all point at the place by identity, so renaming it no longer orphans anything.

Two more, added this round. A visit stores its start and its length and never its end, so a window cannot disagree with itself — which is why every screen that shows 13:30 – 15:45 is computing it. And a loop is a sibling of the visits rather than a child of one: it hangs off the day, names the stop it happens inside, and there can be several.

Firebase: eight steps in your consolecode done

Everything in the app is built and green. The project itself is not something that can be created, or checked, from a sandbox: it needs your Google account. The steps are numbered in WEB_APP_GUIDE.md and take about ten minutes — create the project, register a web app, paste the config, turn on Google and Email link (passwordless), create Firestore in Singapore, paste firebase/firestore.rules, deploy, and sign in once on each phone.

Apple sign-in is not offered, and that is deliberate. On the web it needs a Services ID and a signing key from the Apple Developer Program, which is $99 a year. The rule that nothing costs money has held for eight rounds and it holds here: Google and an emailed link cover everyone, iPhone included.

One thing worth knowing before you do it. Until you sign in, a share link still works — but only on the phone that made it, because the rules have no account to check and nothing is published. That is the same as it has always been. The moment you sign in, the same link starts working everywhere.

How this was checked. Round eight was driven in a browser at 390×844 like the seven before it: 91 new checks across five harnesses, all passing, on top of round seven's 351, with no console errors on any screen. The rules were not read and hoped over — they were run against Google's own rules engine in the Firestore emulator, 26 assertions of it: another account cannot read your trip or a note deep under it; anyone holding a link code can read that one envelope but nobody can list the collection; a stranger cannot publish into it; a guest on an edit link may add their own uid and nothing else, may then send updates, and may not take the envelope over or quietly drop the other editors; a read link does not let them in at all. Sharing was then checked as two separate browser contexts with nothing in common but the envelope — which is the thing the last seven rounds could not do. One made a trip and a link; the other opened /j/CODE having never seen it, read the trip off the envelope, joined, and got its own document in its own account. The owner sent an update, the other side was told without being rewritten, and taking it was its own decision. Switching the link off reached the far phone; a code nobody published is a dead end with words on it rather than a blank screen. Signing in was followed all the way through: the identity keeps its id, so a role, a note and a snapshot written before signing in still point at the same person; a trip made before an account is carried into it under the id it already had; an anonymous user left by the old build is linked rather than replaced, so its uid does not move; signing out keeps the identity and puts the phone back on its own storage. An account with nothing in it was confirmed to get an empty trips home and to have nothing written into it — the sample trip stays on a phone that has never signed in. The new sharing model was checked with two identities on two separate trip documents, which is what it now actually makes: a link was created, joined — and joining produced a genuinely separate trip carrying the itinerary and none of the owner's shopping, packing or Log — then the owner changed a stop, sent an update, and the far side was offered one decision with both versions side by side. Keeping theirs left the day alone; taking theirs wrote it in. The snapshot was inspected directly and carries none of the four private kinds. A trip was exported, read back, and every stop, coordinate, place, sub route, must-see spot, shopping item and note counted on both sides. Three kinds of unreadable file were each refused with their own sentence. A shopping item was corrected in all four fields and moved between places; a must-see spot was added, edited and refused when untitled; a place's table was typed into and an empty row was dropped rather than kept. What that turned up: two bugs, both fixed. The trips home painted before it knew what was in the account, so an account with trips flashed as an empty one. And an invite link would have been a blank page in production: /j/CODE is served the app's own page, so every relative script in it was being resolved against /j/ — the one screen a stranger ever sees, broken by a path. Both are fixed and both are now checked. What that did not cover: the three free services — weather, geocoding, exchange rates — are blocked from this sandbox, so their parsers were verified against recorded payloads rather than live calls. Tiles were served locally rather than from OpenStreetMap. And no real Firebase project was touched: the rules ran against Google's emulator, but the auth and the database the app itself talked to were stand-ins served in place of the SDK. That means the code paths are real and the console is not — Google's own sign-in sheet, the mail that carries a sign-in link, and whether your project has the two providers switched on are exactly the things step 3 of the guide is for, and the first real sign-in is the proof. Still not covered: anything that only shows up on a real trip with real signal, a screen reader on a real phone, and any itinerary shaped differently from the ones I could invent.
