What the Travel Planner still needs

The eight code items from the last round are fixed and deployed. What remains is nineteen things that need a design decision — reframed around the three answers you gave: one real trip, no paid APIs, must work offline.

Start with these three

Everything else can wait a round. These are the ones where designing later means redesigning what came before.

03
Blocker

Nothing can be deleted

Unchanged from round one, and now the only blocker. Itinerary stops can be archived; shopping items, packing items, categories, places, notes, photos and sub-route picks cannot be removed at all. Emptying the whole trip is the one delete that exists, which is a sledgehammer.

On a real trip you will mistype an item within the first hour, and it will still be there on day six.

To design One delete gesture that works on a phone across all the lists — swipe, long-press, or an edit mode per list — and a decision on which lists archive (like the itinerary) and which delete outright. Deletes need an undo; a confirm dialog on every tap gets old within a day.
25
New

"Stop" and "place" are two different things, and it showsnew

This is the deepest problem in the app and it surfaced while fixing the anchoring bugs. An itinerary stop and a nearby place are separate objects. A place knows which stop it hangs off; a stop does not know it is also a place. So:

Adding a nearby place to your itinerary creates a second, unrelated object with the same name. Must-see spots and nearby places attach to stops, but shopping items attach to a place name typed as text — so renaming a stop silently orphans its shopping items. And a stop you typed yourself can never carry opening hours, because only stops matched to a place record have anywhere to put them.

To design One model. My recommendation: everything is a place, and the itinerary is an ordered list of visits to places, each with a time and a route colour. A "sub route" is then just visits you added, and shopping items, shots and notes all hang off the place — which is exactly what the turn-1 write-up promised with "the place is the atom". This is worth drawing as a diagram before any screen.
01
Serious

Getting a real itinerary inchanged

Downgraded from a blocker: you can now set the trip's name, city, dates and length, empty the demo content, and type stops with times. So the app is usable for your trip today.

What is still missing is the part that makes it pleasant. Typing six days of stops on a phone keyboard is a chore, and your itinerary already exists as a PDF or a WhatsApp message. Note that with no paid APIs and no server, parsing has to happen on the device or by hand — so the realistic design is a paste-and-confirm screen, not magic.

To design A paste-the-itinerary screen: you paste text, the app splits it into candidate stops with times, and you confirm or correct each row before it lands in the day. Show the half-understood case, because that is the normal one. A trips list can wait.

The rest, by weight

Numbers carried over from round one so you can cross-reference; 27 and 28 are new because of the offline requirement.

02
Serious

Empty states and the first launchchanged

I wrote plain empty states for Map, Plan, Nearby and Must-see while fixing the anchoring, so the app no longer shows blank screens. They are functional, not designed, and Shop, Prep and Log still have none.

There is also a first-launch question now that this is for one real trip: the app seeds a demo itinerary so the screens have something in them, and you then have to find the settings sheet to empty it. That is backwards.

To design Empty states for Shop, Prep and Log, and a first-run choice: start an empty trip, or look around the demo first. Prep in particular starts completely empty after clearing — worth designing a starter packing list rather than a blank screen.
06
Serious

Times are text, and a stop's window can't be editedchanged

Partly improved: the coach deadline is now read off the anchor stop's window, so the buffer follows the itinerary. That created a new gap — the window ("13:30 – 15:45") is the field that now drives the buffer, and there is no way to edit it. Edit mode only exposes a stop's start time.

Still true from round one: times are free-text boxes, the duration labels are fixed strings rather than anything derived, and dragging a stop can put 09:15 after 18:00 without a word of warning.

To design How a stop's start and end are entered — one time and a duration, or two times — and what an out-of-order or overlapping day looks like. A warning on the row is probably right; refusing the drag is probably wrong.
05
Serious

Free time happens more than once a day

Unchanged. One sub route per day, hanging off one stop. A coach day with an hour at a shrine in the morning and two hours at a market after lunch has nowhere to put the second loop. Now that places are correctly per-stop, this is the remaining half of the same idea.

To design How the Plan shows two or three loops in one day, and how the Sub route screen says which stop you are looping from.
04
Serious

A day holds one note, and place notes overwrite it

Unchanged. "Add a note" on Nishi Market and tapping Day 3 in the Log open the same composer and write to the same record, so the market overwrites the shrine.

To design Notes belong to a stop, and the Log day card stacks that day's notes with the stop name on each. Falls out naturally if item 25 is settled first.
09
Serious

The app knows you'll miss the coach and never tells you

Unchanged, and the highest ratio of usefulness to effort in the list. The buffer sits on a screen you have to remember to open. Same for the 14:00 demo, stalls shutting at 17:00, and the last-chance airport items.

Worth knowing for the design: an installed home-screen web app can send notifications on iOS 16.4 and later, and they work with no server — so this is buildable within your constraints.

To design Where a reminder is set — per stop, per shopping item, or one trip setting — and what it says when it fires.
07
Serious

Where place information comes fromchanged

Your no-paid-APIs answer settles the mechanism: no Google Places, so opening hours, phone numbers and websites cannot be fetched. OpenStreetMap gives a location and sometimes a category, and nothing more reliable than that.

So the confident eight-row table on the destination screen — hours, phone, website, tickets, transport, payment, facilities, language — is a table you fill in by hand, one place at a time. That is fine for the five stops that matter and absurd for forty.

To design Which rows earn their keep when a human types them, and what the table looks like mostly empty — because mostly empty is the normal state. Consider a single free-text "what I need to know" note instead of eight labelled rows for places you added yourself.
08
Serious

Must-see spots can't be added or photographedchanged

Sharper than round one. Now that shots correctly belong to a stop, your real trip has exactly zero of them and no way to add any — the four demo shots belonged to the demo market. You can tick a shot you cannot create.

To design Adding a spot — name, where to stand, best time — attaching a reference image you found, and what the card looks like once your own photo sits beside the reference. Note that without Cloud Storage, photos are 320px thumbnails; the design should not promise full-bleed images.
10
Serious

The shopping list can't be corrected or reorganised

Unchanged. No renaming, no changing an estimate, no moving an item to another shop, no quantity, no reordering. And the by-day grouping you asked for in your original thirteen changes still does not exist — the list groups by place only.

To design An item's edit state, and whether "by place" and "by day" are two views of one list or one grouping with day headers inside each place.
27
New

Offline means the map goes blanknew

The trip data works offline now, but map tiles are images fetched per view. Tiles you have already looked at are cached, so the areas you browsed at the hotel will still draw — but pan somewhere new with no signal and you get grey. Which is exactly the situation the sub route exists for.

Your own prototype anticipated this: "offline map pack" is an item on the demo packing list. It needs designing rather than assuming.

To design A "download this area for offline" action — where it lives, how the area is chosen (the day's stops plus a margin is the honest answer), what it says about size, and what the map shows for an area that was never downloaded.
28
New

You can't tell whether your changes are savednew

There are now three states and the app only shows one of them. Online and syncing: silent, correct. Offline with the cache: edits queue and replay later, also silent — and indistinguishable from the first. Configured but unable to reach Firebase at all: changes are stranded in that one browser, and I added a red banner for it because silence there is dangerous.

That banner is my improvisation, not a design. For an app whose whole point is being used in a market with bad signal, knowing whether your ticked-off shopping list actually saved is not a detail.

To design How the app shows syncing, queued-offline, and stranded — small enough to ignore when all is well, unmissable when it isn't.
11
Serious

You cannot travel with anyone

Unchanged, and still unanswered — you told me the trip is yours but not who else is on it. Sign-in is anonymous, so the trip belongs to one browser: your phone and your laptop hold two unrelated trips, and clearing Safari's data starts a third. Nobody else can tick the packing list.

To design Only if someone else really is coming: sign-in with Apple or Google, and sharing a trip — who can edit, what an invite looks like, whether a shared list shows who ticked what. If it's just you, the one thing still worth doing is sign-in, so a cleared browser doesn't lose the trip.
12
Serious

Bilingual was deferred, and every new screen widens the gap

Unchanged. The turn-2 design carried 中文 throughout; the build is English-only by your choice. Every screen designed from here without Chinese strings is a screen that gets revisited twice.

To design Both languages in any new artboard, and the awkward cases settled: place names that stay in the local language, and a long Chinese label in a chip sized for English.
13
Polish

No dark mode

Unchanged. Light only, and half your use will be at night on a coach or in bed planning tomorrow. The route colours need re-checking on a dark ground rather than inverting — amber on near-black loses the "this is mine" contrast it has on bone.

To design A dark palette for the existing tokens.
16
Polish

The design hasn't had an accessibility passchanged

The payment control is now a proper 32px-plus target, but the rest stands: amber-on-cream chips near the edge of readable contrast, labels at 9.5–10px, and the sort and packed-in controls still under Apple's 44px. It all matters more on a bright street with cold hands than it does at a desk.

To design Minimum sizes and contrast, and a check against iOS larger-text settings.
14
Polish

Nothing is searchable

Unchanged. Fine at fourteen places; not at a hundred, four days of notes and a long packing list.

To design Per-screen or one trip-wide search, and what it returns.
15
Polish

The trip recap is promised but doesn't exist

Unchanged. The Log's black card describes route map, all photos, what you bought, and the places you saved but never reached, carried into the next trip. It is the most appealing idea in the design and it is still a paragraph. Lower priority now that this is one specific trip — but it's what makes a second trip start warm.

To design The recap, and especially the saved-but-never-reached list.

Two things still unanswered

People

Who else is on this trip?

It decides item 11 entirely — whether sharing is a large piece of design work or just sign-in so a cleared browser doesn't lose your itinerary.

Platforms

Is the native iOS app parked?

It has never been compiled and it now trails the web app by every fix in this round. My suggestion stands: leave it, build on the web app, and revisit only if you get a Mac and want it in the App Store. Say so and I'll stop keeping it in mind.

What I'd design in what order

  1. Item 25, the model. One diagram: places, visits, and what hangs off which. Items 4, 5, 7 and 8 all resolve more cleanly once this is settled, and drawing screens first means drawing them twice.
  2. Item 03, deletion. The only blocker, and one gesture covers six screens.
  3. Item 01, pasting an itinerary in. The difference between using the app on this trip and not.
  4. Items 02 and 06 — empty states and how time is entered. Both are small, both are everywhere.
  5. Items 09, 27 and 28 — reminders, offline maps, sync state. The three that make it trustworthy on the actual trip.
  6. The rest once you've used it for a day and know what you missed.
How this was checked. Everything marked fixed was driven in a browser — the whole build-a-real-trip path from emptying the demo to typing an itinerary, plus a 28-step regression across all ten screens. The three free services were verified against recorded API payloads rather than live calls, because this sandbox blocks outbound requests to them; they are the first thing to confirm on the deployed app. New findings (25, 27, 28) came out of doing the work, not from re-reading the design. Still not covered: the native SwiftUI app, which has never been compiled, and anything that only shows up on a real trip with real signal.
